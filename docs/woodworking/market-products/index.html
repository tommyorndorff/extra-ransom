    Top Ten Market Products

    What to build for craft fairs and Christmas markets. The goal is a table that sells: a few cheap impulse items, a solid middle of gift-priced pieces, and one or two “hero” pieces that draw people in.

    Numbers are planning estimates, not market data. Prices, material costs and build times below are my own rough figures for a small one-person shop selling at regional craft markets. Replace them with your own after the first market, using real receipts and a stopwatch. Check booth fees and your state’s sales-tax rules before the first sale. This is not tax advice, so talk to a CPA about how a hobby-versus-business classification affects deductions.

    1. Mitered picture frames

    You already have the dimensioned plan: Mitered Picture Frame, 1¼″ × ⅞″ profile.

    • Cut a standard size run: 5×7, 8×10, 11×14 and 16×20. People buy to a size they already own a photo or print for.
    • Offer two or three species or stains (maple, walnut, cherry or oak). Contrast is what makes a rack of frames read well.
    • Batch the work: mill all stock at once, cut all miters on one stop-block setting, then glue up in sets of four.
    • Sell them with a cheap mat and backer already fitted. Frames with glass sell for more, but glass breaks in transit, so sell glass-free frames with an acrylic option.

    2. Cutting and serving boards

    • Edge-grain boards in maple, walnut and cherry are the easy volume. End-grain boards sell for more but cost more time and glue-up risk.
    • Use food-safe finishes only: mineral oil with beeswax. Say so on a small tag. Avoid exotic woods with irritant dust or unknown food safety.
    • Batch: glue up six to eight blanks at once, then do all the roundovers, handle holes and finishing in one pass.
    • Sell a board with a small tin of board butter as a bundle. The margin on the add-on is high.

    3. Birdhouses and nest boxes

    • Decide whether each one is decorative or functional and label it. A functional nest box needs the right hole size, no perch, ventilation and drain holes, and a way to open it for cleaning. Wren, chickadee and bluebird boxes have different hole diameters, so check a reliable source (the NABS or Audubon plans) before you cut.
    • Cedar or cypress needs no finish and weathers well. Pine plus a nice finish suits the decorative ones.
    • Cut kits from a single template: one pattern board, one stop setting, and a pocket of screws makes these fast.

    4. Wall boxes (“pipe box” / salt box)

    A salt box has a sloped, hinged lid over a small box, and a pipe box has an open rack above a small drawer. Both are the same idea: a small box with a shaped back plate that hangs on a wall.

    • Typical size is about 5–7″ wide, 14–18″ tall and 4–5″ deep, with a scalloped or ogee back and either a hinged lid or an open front.
    • Sell them as candle boxes, salt boxes, key holders or wall storage. The same body can carry four different labels.
    • Vary the back shape (ogee, scallop, flat-top) and sell them in cherry, walnut or painted pine.
    • One jig for the shaped back saves the most time here. A bandsaw template plus a spindle or drum sander gets repeatable curves.

    5. Ornaments and stocking stuffers

    This is the cheap category that earns more than it looks like it should at Christmas markets.

    • Ideas: scroll-saw or laser-cut ornaments, turned or lathe-made tree toppers, wooden spoons and spatulas, bottle stoppers, keychains, coasters, small cheese knives and ring-toss games.
    • Use cutoffs and offcuts from items 1 to 4. This is where the scrap pays for itself.
    • Price in round numbers ($6, $10, $15, $20) so cash and card handling stays fast. Put a basket at the front of the table.

    6. Peg rails and wall shelves

    • Shaker peg rails (a 24–36″ board with 4–6 pegs) are one of the fastest products to make, and they look right on a rail hung on your booth wall.
    • Small ledge shelves, plate rails and key-and-mail racks use the same stock and tooling.
    • Buy turned pegs by the hundred rather than making them. It saves hours.

    7. Step stools

    • A two-step stool in pine or poplar costs little, and a well-built one in oak or ash is a “forever” piece.
    • A kids’ step stool with a hand hole also sells well. Round every edge, check the stability, and keep finishes non-toxic.
    • Design for load: use thick enough stretchers and glue plus screws or dowels at every joint. These items get stood on, so do not skimp.

    8. Small keepsake boxes

    • Keep it simple: butt or mitered corners with splines, a lift-off or hinged lid, and a nice finish. Add a felt or velvet liner if you want a higher price.
    • Sell them in maple, walnut and cherry with contrasting splines. They show your skill and sell as gifts.
    • Time is the cost here, so make only a few. They also work as a showcase for the booth.

    9. Serving trays and totes

    • A flat-bottom serving tray with cut-out handles and a simple tote or caddy (tool, cutlery, garden, picnic) share stock and techniques with the boards.
    • The drawing shows a cheese tray. It makes sense around the holidays: a cheese tray, a coffee-and-tea tray or a wine caddy.

    10. Economical entryway bench

    Your largest piece, and it should be a display anchor, not a volume item. A person who stops for the bench ends up buying a frame as well.

    • Keep it affordable to build: a 36–48″ pine, poplar or ash bench with a plank top, apron and four legs, or a slab top with simple stretchers. About $45–90 in lumber.
    • Use simple, strong joinery: pocket screws plus glue, or mortise-and-tenon if you want to show off. Match the joinery to the price.
    • Build two for the season, not ten. Take photos and offer to build to order in a different finish or size.
    • Related existing plans on this site: the Shaker coffee table and the console plans can be adapted into higher-ticket commissions.

    3D-printed draw-ins and hybrids

    You have a printer, so use it for two jobs: traffic builders that get people to the table, and print + wood hybrids that sit in the gift tier. Print-and-forget items also run while you build everything else.

    Small printed pieces for sale are fine, but check any model licence before selling prints. Many free STLs are personal-use only, so print your own designs (see the parametric tools already on this site) or models whose licence allows commercial use.

    Free or near-free draw-ins

    These cost cents each and give someone a reason to stop and talk.

    • Keep giveaways cheap and fast. Print a batch the night before on a multi-part plate. Count on 2–3× more than you hand out, since some get taken without a conversation.
    • Always attach a card or QR code. A free item with no way back to you is just a cost.
    • One per person, with a sign. “Free fidget with any purchase” protects margin; “free fidget, ask me how it’s made” brings in the curious.
    • Do not skip safety. For anything kids may mouth, avoid tiny loose parts and sharp edges, and sand or deburr every print.

    Making the numbers work

    • Price floor: a common rule of thumb is price ≥ 2–3× materials plus your time at a minimum hourly rate you choose. If an item cannot clear that at a price the market will pay, cut it.
    • Track from day one: log each sale, each material receipt, booth fees, mileage and show-specific costs in one sheet. Good records matter for any tax treatment.
    • Time per unit drops with batches. The times above assume batch work. A one-off takes 2–3× longer.
    • Test first: take about 40 pieces to a small market before investing in a big run, and note what people pick up and what they buy.

    A starting plan for this season

    Christmas markets are weeks away, so start with the quick builds and add the rest after your first market.

    1. Week 1: frames (1), ornaments (5), peg rails (6), and a batch of printed giveaways. Fast, uses stock you already have.
    2. Week 2: boards (2), birdhouses (3), trays (9).
    3. Week 3+: wall boxes (4), step stools (7), and the bench (10) if time allows.

    Open questions

    • Which markets, and what are the booth fees and dates?
    • Is there an existing shop capacity limit (how many hours a week)?
    • Do you want to stay food-safe-only for boards, or also sell decorative ones?
    • Which species can you source cheaply and locally?
