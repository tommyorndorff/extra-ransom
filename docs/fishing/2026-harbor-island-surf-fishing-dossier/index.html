    2026 Harbor Island Dossier: Surf Fishing & Blue Crab

    2026 Harbor Island Dossier: Surf Fishing & Blue Crab

    Trip prep for Oct 1–5, 2026, based at Harbor Island, SC (Beaufort County) — a gated community on St. Helena Sound between St. Helena Island and Hunting Island. Driving down from North Carolina. Shore only: conventional bait and lure, no fly rod, no boat. Primary goal is bull redfish at the north-end point and sandbar, with the pier at Hunting Island, the marsh/creek spots on the island, and the freshwater lagoons as secondary options, and blue crabbing off the community dock as a side quest — plus dolphin watching, which runs on the same low-tide clock but on the marsh side.

    License: non-residents need a SC saltwater recreational fishing license for anything off the Hunting Island pier — $10/day, $35/7-day, or $75/annual. The pier itself is covered by the park’s commercial license, so no individual license is needed to fish from it. Buy online at dnr.sc.gov before the trip.

    Tides: Fripps Inlet (NOAA Station 8668498)

    This is the nearest NOAA station — officially named “Fripps Inlet” (with an s), commonly called Fripp Inlet locally — and covers the inlet bordering Harbor Island’s north end and Hunting Island’s north beach.     A ~6.5–7.5 ft mixed semidiurnal range (two uneven highs and lows a day) — big water by East Coast standards. Smaller range, less current, lighter leads (3 oz usually holds). Surf and inlet-mouth fishing aren’t meaningfully affected by that either way, and the north-end sandbar still fully exposes at low water.

    Reading it for the north end — fish the low, not the high. The local pattern here for bull reds in the surf is two hours before to two hours after low tide, ideally overlapping sunrise or late afternoon — counterintuitive if you’re used to fishing the push of an incoming tide, but at low water on this bar the fish concentrate into the troughs and the channel edge instead of spreading out over open sand. Cast into the seam where the bar drops off, not open water.

    Spots, ranked

    1. North-end point and sandbar (PRIMARY) — inlet mouth, bar, current seam. Peak bull-red season is Sept–Nov here.
    2. Community crabbing dock (marsh side) — blue crabs on chicken necks; also flounder, reds, and trout on a Carolina rig with live shrimp or mud minnow on a moving tide.
    3. Harbor River corridor — sheepshead/black drum on structure, reds/trout on channel edges, but see the caveat below: the accessible piling structure this depends on may no longer exist.
    4. Freshwater lagoons (5 on the island) — check community rules first; alligator habitat; needs a separate freshwater license.
    5. Hunting Island (2 mi away, worth one session) — the pier, and Boneyard Beach at the south end.

    Harbor Island: north end surf fishing (primary goal)

    The stretch of beach running north from Harbor Island toward Fripp Inlet, walkable along the sand — no boat, no kayak, shore only. Early October sits inside the fall bull-redfish spawn through Port Royal Sound, so this is good timing.

    Hazards: sharks working the bar, rays on the flats (shuffle your feet, don’t step down), and a rising tide can cut off the walk back if you’re out past the point when it turns.

    Target species

    • Redfish (bull reds spawning through the sound into October, plus slot fish working the surf)
    • Whiting / kingfish (steady, easy bite in the surf trough)
    • Black drum
    • Flounder
    • Sharks (blacktip, bonnethead — expect incidental hookups on cut bait)

    Realistic table fare from this whole trip, not just the north end: whiting, croaker, black drum, sheepshead, pompano.

    Rigs & bait

    • Carolina rig or fish-finder rig, cut mullet or shrimp — the workhorse setup for redfish and black drum in the inlet current
    • Smaller hooks (#4–1) with shrimp, FishBites, or sand fleas/mole crabs for whiting and pompano in the surf trough
    • Heavier leader (40–60 lb mono/fluoro) if targeting sharks intentionally

    Access notes

    • Walk the beach north from Harbor Island toward the inlet; no marked public access point beyond the island’s own beach access — treat it as a beach walk, not a trailhead.
    • Non-resident saltwater license required (see above) — the pier exemption does not apply to open-beach surf fishing.

    Red drum rules (changed July 1, 2026 — confirmed current for this trip):

    • Slot limit is now 18–25", tightened from the old 15–23" slot.
    • 1 fish per person per day (down from 2), and boats are capped at 2/day (down from 6).
    • Any natural-bait hook 4/0 or larger — which covers the bull-red rig above — must be non-offset, non-stainless circle hook, for red drum, cobia, and tarpon alike.

    Hunting Island: pier and Boneyard Beach (ranked #5, one session)

    2 miles from Harbor Island — worth a single dedicated session rather than a daily rotation.

    The pier. 950 ft into Fripp Inlet on Hunting Island’s south end near the lighthouse, ~28 ft of water at the end. Rebuilt/reopened September 2021 after Hurricane Matthew damage — currently in good condition as of 2026, no known outstanding closures.

    • License: none needed — the park’s commercial license covers all pier anglers, the one exemption to the saltwater license requirement on this whole trip.
    • Species: redfish, speckled trout, flounder, sheepshead (work it vertically on the pilings), black drum.
    • Park hours: 6am–6pm (extended to 9pm during Daylight Saving Time — still in effect Oct 1–5).
    • Entrance fee: confirmed via the official park FAQ as $8/adult (16+); gate opens 6am.
    • Contact: 843-838-2011, southcarolinaparks.com/hunting-island.

    Boneyard Beach, on Little Hunting Island at the south end — the famous stand of submerged/downed trees on the beach, fronting an offshore sandbar with its own trough. Same bull-red potential as the Harbor Island north end (reported fish to 48"), same low-tide logic applies. Good pairing with the pier session since they’re both on the south end of the park, a short walk apart.

    Good rainy-day or dawn-window-missed backup to the north-end plan — fishes fine outside the tight low-tide window that matters most on the open beach.

    Harbor River corridor and freshwater lagoons (ranked #3 and #4)

    Harbor River corridor — verify before counting on this one. The Harbor River separates St. Helena Island from Harbor Island, and its creeks are known redfish water. But the US-21 crossing itself — the actual “Harbor River bridge” — was demolished and replaced in April 2021 with a new fixed high-level span (65 ft vertical / 90 ft horizontal clearance, 3,353 ft long, built for shrimp-boat and sailboat clearance underneath); the old 1939 swing bridge, whose pilings would have been the fishable structure, was towed offshore and sunk as the SC Memorial Reef, ~52 miles out. The new bridge does have 10-ft paved shoulders behind parapet walls on each side — walkable/bikeable in a pinch — but that’s not a dedicated pedestrian path or fishing platform, so don’t count on it as a fishing spot the way the old bridge’s pilings worked. If bank/pier fishing on this river system is the goal, the closer real public option is Roger Pinckney Park on Pigeon Point Road in Beaufort — a small public dock with benches and parking, but it overlooks the Beaufort River / Brickyard Creek confluence, not the Harbor River itself, and it’s a 10–15 mile drive toward Beaufort, not walkable from Harbor Island. Confirm with the gatehouse or on arrival whether there’s still a community dock or bank spot on the Harbor River side before planning around “the bridge” specifically.

    Freshwater lagoons — check first. Five lagoons on Harbor Island itself. Two open questions before fishing them: whether community rules restrict fishing on them at all, and that they’re alligator habitat (act accordingly — no wading, mind pets and kids at the bank). They also require a separate freshwater license ($11/14-day nonresident) — the saltwater license does not cover them.

    Fallback water off-island: St. Helena Sound and the Morgan River hold redfish, speckled trout, flounder, and black drum along oyster-lined banks and creek mouths if both the Harbor River and the lagoons fall through, though most of that leans toward needing a kayak or boat rather than bank access — which conflicts with the shore-only plan for this trip and would need to be an explicit exception if pursued.

    Side quest: blue crabbing

    South Carolina makes casual crabbing easy — no license needed for the simplest methods.

    • License-free methods: up to 3 chicken-neck handlines (no hooks, one bait per line) or up to 3 fold-up traps/drop nets, per person.
    • Crab pots: 2 pots without an endorsement; up to 5 pots with the $5/year recreational crab trap endorsement (requires holding the base saltwater license).
    • Daily limit: 1 bushel per person, capped at 2 bushels per boat.
    • Escape rings: required, at least 2⅜" — single-chamber pots need one, two-chamber pots need two (at least one in the upper chamber).
    • Where: the community crabbing dock on the marsh side of Harbor Island (ranked #2 above) — classic chicken-neck-off-a-dock water, no boat required.

    Source: SC DNR crustacean/shellfish regulations, via eRegulations (the DNR’s own CrabSOL PDF link is currently 404ing — re-pull directly from dnr.sc.gov if a primary-source copy is needed).

    Side quest: best chances at seeing dolphins

    Beaufort County holds roughly 170 full-time resident bottlenose dolphins, and the salt-marsh estuary that threads Hunting Island, Fripp Island, and Harbor Island (the Story River / Johnson Creek / Harbor River network) is working habitat, not a migration corridor they pass through. They’re here year-round. October is the good end of the year for it: the fall mullet run peaks Sept–Nov, concentrating bait into exactly the inlets and creek bottlenecks the dolphins hunt — the same mullet going on the bull-red rig as cut bait. Mid-Atlantic/Lowcountry accounts put Oct–Dec as prime for watching.

    Taxonomy footnote, since it’ll come up: the shallow-water dolphins here were split off as a separate species in 2023 — Tamanend’s bottlenose dolphin (Tursiops erebennus), smaller than the offshore common bottlenose, with vertebral columns adapted to tight estuarine water, and NOAA now assesses them as their own stock. Anything seen from the beach or a creek bank on this trip is almost certainly erebennus.

    Two very different things are on the table, and they should be planned separately:

    1. Ordinary dolphin activity — rolling, traveling, and feeding pods in Fripp Inlet and the creeks. Near-certain over five days if you spend any time looking at water. This needs no plan beyond binoculars.
    2. Strand feeding — the Lowcountry specialty, and a genuine lottery ticket from shore. Plan for it, but do not expect it.

    Strand feeding: what it is, and the tide

    A coordinated group — typically 2–3 dolphins, documented from 2 to 8 — herds a school of mullet against a sloped mud bank, then charges in unison, throwing a bow wave that flings the fish up onto the mud, and beaches itself far enough to pick them off before wriggling back into the water. High-risk, high-skill, learned behavior. First documented in 1971 in the Georgia marshes, and on the entire US East Coast it is only known from the salt-marsh creeks of South Carolina and Georgia. Dolphins do it lying on their right side, consistently — the reason is still unsettled.

    It requires three things at once: a mid-size creek, a sloped mud bank, and low water. That last one is the planning lever, and sources disagree on it:

    Tide-stage conflict, resolved toward low. The research literature and the long-running Kiawah observations both put strand feeding at low tide, with the productive window running roughly two hours either side of the low — the stretch where the creek channel is at its shallowest and narrowest. One Beaufort charter blog instead claims dolphins “typically reserve this technique for when the tide is rising,” and calls falling tides “much less common.” Weight the research: plan around low water. Either way, the two-hours-either-side-of-low window covers both readings.

    That is the same window the north-end bull-red plan already runs on — but strand feeding happens on the marsh side, not on the open ocean beach. Standing at the inlet at dead low buys nothing extra here. It’s a separate errand on a creek bank, and it needs its own slot.

    Where to watch from, ranked

    1. Hunting Island marsh boardwalk — a quarter-mile boardwalk out into a salt-marsh hummock, ending on a dock platform over the creek. South end of the park, past the main beach gate on the right off US-21. The park and Friends of Hunting Island both list dolphins among what’s visible from it, and it’s widely called the best sunset spot in the Lowcountry. This is the single best strand-feeding vantage available on foot on this trip: elevated, over a creek, with mud banks that expose at low water. Fiddler crabs on the bank at low tide confirm you’re looking at the right kind of mud. National Recreation Trail, easy, leashed dogs OK.
    2. Harbor Island community crabbing dock (marsh side) — you’re already standing here for the blue crabs, on a creek with mud banks, and it costs nothing extra. Work the falling tide into the low rather than showing up at dead low. Best odds-per-effort spot on the whole list, because the time is already spent.
    3. Hunting Island fishing pier — 950 ft into Fripp Inlet, ~28 ft at the end; dolphins routinely roll past it on the tide. Not strand-feeding water, but the most reliable see-a-dolphin spot, and the pier session is already on the plan. Pairs with the boardwalk — both are on the park’s south end.
    4. Hunting Island lagoon — calm salt water a short walk off the beach, away from the crowd; park staff reportedly point visitors here for dolphins.
    5. Harbor Island north end / Fripp Inlet — where you’ll be fishing anyway. Dolphins work the inlet mouth and the bar edge on the tide; forum reports of a pod moving 150 yards off the north end of Hunting Island. Free look while the rods sit in the sand spikes.
    6. Johnson Creek Bridge / Harbor River bridge shoulders — high vantage straight over creek and mud bank. The replacement Harbor River span has 10-ft paved shoulders behind parapet walls, but see the caveat above: it is not a pedestrian facility, there’s no parking, and stopping on the span is a bad idea. Filed as “if you happen to be crossing at low water, look.”

    Honest expectation-setting. The research treats Captain Sam’s Spit at Kiawah — about two hours north — as the only place strand feeding can be reliably observed without a boat, which is why the Lowcountry Marine Mammal Network staffs an educator there four hours a day at low tide from April 1 through December 31. Around Harbor Island, strand feeding is opportunistic: real, reported, and not something to build a morning around. Treat a sighting as luck. If it’s a must-see rather than a nice-to-have, it’s a separate Kiawah day trip, not a Harbor Island item.

    Rules, and not being the problem

    Feeding or harassing wild marine mammals is a federal offense under the Marine Mammal Protection Act — cited fines up to $100,000. The practical rules:

    • Distance: NOAA’s southeast guidance is 50 yards from dolphins by vessel; LMMN’s land-based strand-feeding guidance is 15 yards on land. Don’t close the gap “just for a photo.”
    • At a strand-feeding bank, hold still. Walking toward the bank scatters the mullet and the dolphins abort the run. Stay put, stay quiet, let it happen. Documented consequence at Captain Sam’s: heavy onlooker pressure measurably raises stress and changes behavior.
    • Never toss bait or cut-up fish off the pier or the crabbing dock to a dolphin. That’s the standard way anglers create a begging animal, and it’s the same federal offense as hand-feeding.
    • From a boat or kayak: no chasing, encircling, or leapfrogging; never put the hull between a mother and calf.
    • Stranded or dead animal: SCDNR wildlife stranding hotline, 1-800-922-5431.

    Field cues — what actually gives it away

    • Birds first. Excited gulls, herons, and egrets pinned to one stretch of bank are the tell — they drop in for the leftovers. Scan birds, not water.
    • Fresh slide marks in the mud at a bank mean it’s an active feeding bank. Note where, come back on the next low.
    • Showering mullet or nervous water pushed against a bank.
    • A group traveling in a line that suddenly turns as one toward the bank — that’s the commit.
    • They feed on their right side, so a given bank gets worked in a consistent direction. Pick a promising bank and stay on it rather than roaming the boardwalk.
    • No time-of-day preference beyond the tide — it happens day and night. Frequency is wildly variable: Kiawah observations range from once or twice a day to 37 times in a day.

    Gear delta

    One real addition: binoculars, 8x42 — the handheld sweet spot, and dual-use for reading birds working bait in the surf. Everything else is already on the list (polarized sunglasses do most of the work). Phone video of a distant bank is not worth reorganizing a morning around.

    Day plan

    • Day 1, dead low: walk the north end with no gear — map troughs, cuts, and bar edges. Polarized sunglasses, wading shoes, water, phone. Worth more than any tackle decision made in advance. Crab off the dock that afternoon.
    • Each morning, low near sunrise (strongest Oct 1–3): north-end point, both anglers — Rod A working cut mullet in the current seam, Rod B working the trough with shrimp/FishBites for whiting and pompano.
    • One session: Hunting Island — pier for guaranteed variety, plus a look at Boneyard Beach. Add the marsh boardwalk on the same ticket (same end of the park) for dolphins.
    • Oct 4 and Oct 5 mornings — the dolphin slots: these are the weakest surf mornings (low has drifted off sunrise) and the two strongest marsh-side windows, entirely in daylight. Spend one of them on the boardwalk or the crabbing dock instead of the beach.
    • Oct 1 evening: only strong evening window for either objective — low at 6:26p on the beach, 6:39p on the Harbor River, with light until ~7:07p. Fish it or watch it, not both.
    • Wind contingency: beach blown out → the marsh side or, if the Harbor River access question above resolves favorably, sheltered water and oyster edges there instead. A blown-out beach is also a fine excuse to go sit on the marsh boardwalk.
    • Every night, non-negotiable: hose both reels with fresh water and back the drags off. The Pursuit IV is not a sealed reel — this five-minute habit is the whole reason the cheaper reels are the right call (see the reel note above).

    Gear checklist

    Oct 1–5, 2026 · shore fishing, conventional · neap tides. Organized by when you need to act: buy before leaving → pack → buy on the way in → do before you go.

    Buy before leaving — rod, reel, line (two anglers, two different outfits)

    Custom build is off the table (no time). Two-piece for the drive down. Two rods, deliberately not identical, so two people can fish the north end at the same time without both being tied to the same rig — one heavy outfit for the bull-red/inlet-seam work, one lighter outfit for trough and pier fishing.

    • Rod A — bull reds, inlet seam (PRIMARY): Penn Prevail III, 10’ Medium-Heavy, 2-piece — 1–5 oz, 15–30 lb. The 9’ Medium caps at 3 oz, too light for a 4 oz Sputnik plus a fist-sized mullet chunk, and no 9’ MH exists in the Prevail III or Airwave Elite lineups.
    • Reel A: Penn Pursuit IV, 6000 — ~$70. Graphite body, HT-100 drag, 20 lb max drag, 490 yds of 30 lb braid, 5.6:1, 21.4 oz, 5 bearings. Matched to Rod A for bulls. (Penn sizes these 6000/5000 rather than the Spinfisher’s 6500/5500 — same class of reel.)
    • Rod B — whiting/pompano/sheepshead, lighter work: Penn Prevail III, 9’ Medium, 2-piece — 3/4–3 oz, 10–20 lb. This is the same rod family/spec that was previously rejected as a second rod for one person to carry — it’s the right call now that it belongs to a second angler instead of doubling one person’s load. Doubles as the Hunting Island pier rod.
    • Reel B: Penn Pursuit IV, 5000 — ~$70. Same body and drag, 20 lb max, 300 yds of 30 lb braid, 19.7 oz (4 sealed bearings + instant anti-reverse); better balance on the lighter blank.
    • Braid, 30 lb, two spools — Power Pro or Suffix 832
    • Two hard rod tubes, if the rods ride on a roof rack or in a truck bed

    Rod fallbacks if the Prevail III 10’ MH is out of stock: Tsunami Airwave Elite 10'6" MH, Ugly Stik Bigwater 9’ MH (composite, heavier, near-unbreakable), or the Bass Pro house brands (Offshore Angler Breakwater / Ocean Master) on a budget. For Rod B: Tsunami Airwave Elite 9’ M, or an Ugly Stik Bigwater 7’ MH sized down.

    Reel fallbacks if Pursuit IV sizes are out: Penn Fierce IV 6000/5000 ($108 each, metal frame — see below), Okuma Avenger B, or Daiwa BG 5000. Penn Battle III 6000 ($145+) is the ceiling worth considering and still well under the Spinfisher.

    Explicitly rejected: the MHX SU1088F-2 custom build (an exact spec match, but no time before Oct 1 and Mud Hole has reported component stock problems).

    Why not the Spinfisher VII. An earlier draft of this list specced the Spinfisher VII 6500 and 5500 — roughly $240 and $226, about $466 for the pair. What that premium actually buys is IPX5 sealing of the body, spool, and drag. That is the right call for someone in the salt 40+ days a year. At one or two trips a year it’s insurance you’ll never collect on. The Pursuit IV pair lands around $140 — call it $326 saved — and the things it gives up are mostly things this trip doesn’t use:

    • Drag was never the constraint. A bull red gets fought at 8–12 lb of drag. The Pursuit IV’s 20 lb is still roughly double anything you’d set; the Spinfisher’s 30 lb was pure headroom.
    • Neither was capacity. The 6000 carries 490 yds of 30 lb braid — identical to the metal-bodied Fierce IV, and far more than a red is going to take off the reel at the inlet.
    • Weight is the least of it here. 21.4 oz is heavy for a spinning reel, but these sit in sand spikes most of the morning rather than getting cast continuously.

    Two real tradeoffs, stated plainly:

    1. Graphite body, not metal. This is the actual compromise. A graphite frame flexes under heavy static load in a way a metal one doesn’t, and repeated flex is what walks gear alignment out of true. The practical rule that follows: never crank a buried Sputnik out of the sand with the reel. Point the rod at it and pull the braid by hand, or break it off — that dead-weight load, not a fish, is what kills budget reels.
    2. It is not a sealed reel. The “sealed stainless ball bearings” refers to the bearings. So the nightly fresh-water rinse stops being optional — it’s in the day plan for that reason. That five-minute habit is what the $326 buys back.

    If the metal frame turns out to matter: Penn Fierce IV 6000 and 5000, $108 each ($216 the pair) — full metal body and sideplates, HT-100 carbon-fiber drag, 25 lb max, same 490/30 capacity on the 6000. It’s the cheapest metal-bodied reel in the Penn line and the honest step up from here, at roughly $76 more for the pair. Worth it if these reels are going to see more than the one or two trips a year they’re being bought for. Either way, do not substitute the Penn Wrath II 6000 ($79.99) — it costs more than the Pursuit IV and runs 3 bearings to the Pursuit’s 5.

    Prices are mid-September 2026 street, not list — Penn’s own list across the Pursuit IV range runs $84.99–$109.99, so the ~$70 figure assumes a normal retailer discount. Re-check before ordering, and buy the pair from one retailer to save on shipping.

    Buy before leaving — terminal tackle

    Buy ~3x what seems reasonable — inlet current and oyster bottom eat tackle.

    • Fluorocarbon leader: 20 lb, 30 lb, 50 lb (bulls and toothy fish)
    • Mono 50 lb, for fishfinder rig components
    • Hooks — non-offset, non-stainless circle only, required by law with natural bait at 4/0+ (see the red drum rule above): 6/0–8/0 for bull reds, 2/0–3/0 for drum and slot reds, #1–1/0 for whiting/pompano/sheepshead
    • Sputnik sinkers, 3 oz and 4 oz — wire legs grip and release; the 10’ MH handles both. 4 oz for the inlet seam, 3 oz for the beach — and with this trip landing near neap, 3 oz will usually hold on its own.
    • Pyramid sinkers, 3 oz (backup); egg sinkers, 1/2–2 oz (Carolina rigs on the dock); split shot (vertical sheepshead on the pier pilings)
    • Pre-tied two-hook pompano rigs (6+) — cheaper than components
    • Plastic sinker sliders, beads, barrel swivels; jigheads, 1/8 and 1/4 oz
    • Popping corks (Cajun Thunder style) for the marsh side; Gulp shrimp, new penny

    Pack — tools

    Sand spikes (2), long-nose pliers, dehooker, braid scissors/line clippers, fish grippers, bump board or tape measure (verify the 18–25" red drum slot before keeping), tackle backpack or 5-gal bucket with lid seat, headlamp with red mode, rag/hand towel, collapsible crab trap or hand lines, small cooler + ice, flow-through or aerated bucket for mud minnows, binoculars (8x42) — dolphins off the marsh boardwalk, and birds working bait in the surf.

    Pack — clothing and comfort

    Polarized sunglasses — highest-value item on the list for reading the bar, troughs, and seams. Also: long-sleeve sun shirt, wide-brim hat, high-SPF sunscreen, quick-dry pants/shorts, old sneakers or wading shoes (not barefoot near oysters), picaridin or DEET (no-see-ums on the marsh side at dawn/dusk are serious), light rain shell (early-Oct afternoon cells), water and food (nothing available at the north end), phone in a dry bag or case.

    Buy on the way in — bait

    Nothing is available on Harbor Island itself — buy on St. Helena Island or in Beaufort: fresh or frozen shrimp (the universal bait here); cut mullet (bulls and sharks — fresh substantially outperforms frozen); mud minnows (flounder and reds); FishBites, sand-flea flavor (won’t spoil — whiting and pompano); chicken necks from a grocery store (crabbing); fiddler crabs if available (sheepshead off the Hunting Island pier).

    Before you go — admin

    • SC nonresident saltwater license, 7-day $35 — buy via Go Outdoors South Carolina, save to phone (not required on the Hunting Island pier — see above)
    • Freshwater license, $11/14-day, only if the island’s lagoons turn out to be fishable
    • Verify red drum limits on SCDNR before the trip in case the July 1, 2026 rules get revised further
    • Pull NOAA tide predictions for Harbor River / St. Helena Sound too (not just Fripp Inlet) and note which day puts low near sunrise; download an offline tide chart — done for Harbor River Bridge (8668146) and Johnson Creek Bridge (8668227), see the dolphin section
    • Call the gatehouse: beach/dock fishing rules, north-end parking and access, lagoon rules
    • Look up bait shop hours on St. Helena Island
    • Cash for Hunting Island park entry — $8/adult, gates open 6am

    Day-one carry (north end, dead-low recon)

    Deliberately minimal — the recon walk is worth more than any tackle decision: polarized sunglasses, wading shoes, water, phone for marking troughs, cuts, and seams. No rods.

    • Call Coastal Expeditions at (843) 884-7684 to confirm the Hunting Island / Story River dolphin cruise is running Oct 1–5 and grab a slot — the site publishes no season, and the St. Helena outpost is on the drive in.
