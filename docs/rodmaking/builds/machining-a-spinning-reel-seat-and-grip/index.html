    Machining a Spinning Reel Seat and Building a Grip

    A plan and procedure for making the handle hardware of Rod 002, the 6'6" French Broad spinning rod myself: a machined reel seat (fixed hood plus a sliding threaded ring) and a cork grip built around it. The build log already covers two handle options: cork with two sliding rings (Option A) and a Fuji VSS skeleton seat on an arbor (Option B). This page is a third option: turn the seat from bar stock instead of buying it.

    Status: plan only. Nothing here has been cut yet. The cane for Rod 002 hasn’t been milled either. Anything I haven’t measured or confirmed is marked to confirm, and every dimension below is a starting point, not a result.

    Choosing the style

    StyleHow it holds the reelProsConsFit for Rod 002
    Sliding rings on cork (Option A in the log)Foot sits on bare cork, two rings slide over the endsLightest; period-correct; no hardwareRings can creep when wet and coldRecommended in the log
    Fixed hood + threaded ring (this page)Foot sits in a fixed hood; a threaded ring winds up against the other footLocks solid; no creep; adjustable to the reelNeeds machining and exact thread fitThe one planned here
    Hood and cap, no thread (slip-on sliding band)Same as the sliding rings but with metal-to-metal hoodsSimple to makeSame creep problem as ringsSkip
    Bought skeleton seat (Fuji VSS 16, Option B)Fixed hood plus a threaded hood and lock nutCheap, tested, ~$6Looks modern on cane; needs an arborFallback

    A screw-lock seat (what the machined design is) beats a sliding band on a spinning rod because the reel foot takes the full pull of a hooked fish and the casting hand squeezes the seat. The band-only style is fine for light fly-style reels but is the one more likely to loosen.

    Spinning-specific geometry

    • The reel hangs below the seat. The foot channel is open on the underside. The seat is a tube with a flat-bottomed (or slightly dished) cradle where the foot sits, a fixed hood at one end, and a threaded ring at the other that pulls the second foot tight.
    • Seat body OD: 7/8″ to 1″ (22–25 mm) is the usual range for finished spinning seats and the size my hand is likely to want on a light rod. Finished seat sizes vary by maker, so I’ll settle on a diameter from the reel and my grip (to confirm).
    • Reel foot gap. The distance between the fixed hood and the threaded ring at full open must exceed the foot-to-foot length of the reel, and at full closed must be less than the foot length. I’ll measure the 1000-size reel I’m going to use with calipers (to confirm) and make the channel about 1/4″ longer than that with the threaded ring’s travel at least that much.
    • Hex under a round bore. The butt is 0.279–0.281″ across flats, so the corners are 0.281 / cos 30° = 0.325″ (8.24 mm). A round bore smaller than that cuts into the corners. Mill the bore to clear the corners, or ream the bushing bore to the corner diameter and fill the gap (see Bushings).

    Threads

    I didn’t find a published thread standard for spinning reel seat nuts. Manufacturers’ product pages (the Mud Hole pages I read) list length, ID, and OD but not the thread pitch, and different makers use different threads. So a machined seat can’t borrow a standard; I choose my own and both mating parts are mine.

    • Pick a common machinist thread so the tooling is on hand: 1″-20 UNEF (1″ major diameter, 20 tpi) is a good candidate for a ~1″ body. The thread depth is about 0.0325″, so the wall stays healthy. To confirm against the taps, dies, and single-point inserts I actually own.
    • Cut the male thread on the lathe (single-point, with the lathe geared for 20 tpi) rather than with a die. A die on 1″ aluminum cuts a rough thread and leaves it off-center. Take light passes and use cutting fluid.
    • Cut the ring’s female thread on the lathe too (boring bar or internal threading insert), then fit it to the body by sneaking up on the thread: stop when it runs freely with no radial shake.
    • Add relief at the end of the thread, so the ring can run up against the shoulder without bottoming on a half-cut thread.
    • Lock it: a thin nylon washer or a small O-ring under the ring’s face, or a second lock ring, so it doesn’t back off. To confirm which works.

    Machining sequence

    This is general machinist practice. I haven’t followed an opened reference for it (see Sources), so treat it as a plan to be checked on scrap first.

    Lathe

    1. Cut bar stock (6061, about 1.25″ diameter, 5″ long) with about 1/2″ extra.
    2. Chuck it, face the end, and drill and bore through to the bore diameter. Bore first so the bore and the outside are concentric.
    3. Put the body on a mandrel (or chuck on the bore) and turn the outside to OD, leaving a shoulder for the fixed hood.
    4. Turn the thread relief, then single-point the male thread.
    5. Part off. Flip, face to length.
    6. Turn the threaded ring: bore, internal thread, then knurl or a hex OD if I want grip.
    7. Test-fit the ring on the body. Fit it before any other cuts.

    Mill (if available)

    1. Cut the foot channel: a flat-bottomed slot across the bed width, with a ball-end or corner radius to match the foot.
    2. Open the underside window so the reel foot can sit on the cane or bushing side.
    3. Drill any vent or pin holes (a small hole to relieve epoxy, and any set-screw I want).

    If I don’t have a mill: cut the channel on the lathe with a slotting setup, or file it by hand with a jig. To confirm.

    Finishing

    OptionProsCons
    Hard-coat or color anodize (aluminum)Tough; many colorsNeeds a vendor or a home kit; coat is thick, so the threads must be cut oversize/undersize to allow for it (to confirm with the anodizer)
    Bead-blast, then clear lacquerEasy; matte lookWears at the thread
    Polish (brass)Classic on caneTarnishes; needs lacquer or regular wax
    Leave bare, clear-coat only on non-thread surfacesWorksNeeds periodic cleaning

    Anodizing adds a few ten-thousandths per surface, so cut the threads and fit them before anodizing, then re-check the fit. Masking the threads during anodizing is the usual alternative.

    Bushings and bonding to the blank

    1. Fit the seat to the cane first, dry. Slide the seat over the butt. It should go on freely at the corners with no rocking.
    2. Make bushings (two, one at each end of the seat): aluminum or brass rings, or turned from epoxy-filled phenolic, with a bore that matches the cane’s taper at that point and an OD that matches the seat bore. Where the bore is round and the blank is hex, either fill the gaps with thickened epoxy or turn tape-and-epoxy bushings in place.
    3. Mask the cane above and below the bonding area, and the seat’s threads and channel, with painter’s tape.
    4. Epoxy with a slow-cure (30-minute or longer) 2-part epoxy. equipment.md records a recommendation of 3M DP2216 for ferrule glue; I could use the same adhesive here (to confirm it suits an aluminum-to-cane bond; the repo only recommends it for ferrules). Use a thin film, and keep it off the threads.
    5. Align the seat so the reel hangs on the same side as the guides, then hold it square with the blank for the cure. A tape alignment mark on the butt helps.
    6. Wipe squeeze-out with denatured alcohol before it gels.

    The grip

    Cork rings vs. shaped cork

    OptionProsCons
    Cork rings, glued up and turnedCheap; pick the best rings for the high-touch spots; any shapeTakes time; voids need filling
    Pre-shaped cork grip (the Option B parts in the log: Fuji split fore/rear grips)Ready to ream and glueLimited shapes; sized for a particular seat

    I’m using rings here since the machined seat doesn’t match a commercial grip.

    Ring dimensions and count

    • Standard ring: 1 1/4″ OD, 1/2″ thick, 1/4″ bore. That’s the size Mud Hole’s overstock cork rings list (page opened). Sizes and grades vary by maker; the “grade” label is a seller’s, not a standard.
    • Rear grip: 7″ is the build log’s choice for the Option B rear grip, about 14 rings at 1/2″. To confirm for this rod (the handle is 12″ total in Option A).
    • Foregrip: about 3.75″ (as in the log), about 7-8 rings.
    • A 1 1/4″ ring leaves about 0.125–0.25″ for shaping around a 1″ seat, so grips that are bigger than the seat are possible but not by much. Larger rings exist; to confirm stock.
    • Buy a few extras and hand-pick: put the best rings where the thumb and palm sit, and the worst at the ends where washers dent them.

    Glue-up

    1. Bore each ring to just under the blank (a tapered rattail file or reamer on a drill) and put them on a waxed mandrel or threaded rod. Rings arrive with a 1/4″ bore; the cane is 0.28–0.33″ at the grip, so ring bores should be opened slightly.
    2. Glue sparingly. Enough that the inner surface just shines; excess glue forces epoxy onto the mandrel and the grip’s outside, and the grip can become nearly impossible to remove.
    3. Press the stack: washers and nuts on threaded rod, or the printable cork press from this site. Tighten until glue just oozes between the rings.
    4. Put a low-grade ring at each end to absorb the press marks.
    5. Cure fully, overnight and a bit more. Reaming too soon makes the grip come apart.

    Turning and shaping

    • On a lathe or a drill with a support stand: true the stack to a cylinder with 60-grit on a flat board, then rough the profile, leaving a little extra, then work up through the grits. One builder ends at 320 grit.
    • Shaping without a lathe: fold sandpaper into a firm corner and work wells into the spinning grip; a rasp for the first roughing.
    • Calipers at the high points; stop slightly above the final diameter.
    • Dust: cork dust is fine and gets everywhere. Work outdoors or with a shop vacuum at the grip.

    Fitting and fill

    • Remove the grip from the mandrel by gripping it with both hands and striking the mandrel on a flat, solid surface.
    • Ream to the blank and test-fit, then bond with epoxy, as with the seat. Put the rear grip on first against the seat’s rear edge and the foregrip against the fixed hood.
    • Fill voids after shaping and before the final sanding: a cork dust and epoxy (or wood-filler) paste, pushed in, left proud, sanded flush.
    • Burnish with a hard smooth surface or a very fine paper (400-grit or finer) to close the surface. (General practice, not from a cited source.)
    • Winding check (in the log’s Option B notes: for a roughly 7.3 mm blank): fit at the front of the foregrip, then wrap and finish.
    • Finish: leave the cork bare or seal it lightly, per taste. To confirm.

    Troubleshooting

    SymptomLikely causeFix
    Ring binds or won’t start on the bodyThread over-cut, or chips in the thread, or the anodize added thicknessClean; re-chase; ease the thread after anodizing
    Ring loosens when wetNo lock; thread too coarse for the loadAdd an O-ring or nylon washer; add a lock ring
    Seat wobbles on the blankBushings oversize or too thinRe-fit; thicken the epoxy; recheck alignment while curing
    Seat won’t slide on over the hex cornersBore smaller than 0.325″ across cornersBore to 0.340″+ or use bushings
    Grip glue line showsToo much glue between the ringsUse less; sand back a little
    Grip cracks on reamingReamed before cure; too fastWait; ream in steps
    Voids in the finished corkPoor ringsFill and sand; move low-grade rings to the ends
    Reel rattles in the seatChannel too long or the hood too shallowShim with tape, or recut with a tighter channel
    Cork dust everywhereDry sandingOutdoors or with a vacuum

    Safety

    • Lathe PPE: safety glasses or face shield; no loose sleeves, gloves, or jewelry; long hair tied back; chuck key out before power on. Run the lathe at a sensible speed for the stock size, and stand out of the line of the chuck. Remove burrs and sharp edges before handling parts.
    • Threading: cut aluminum with light passes and coolant; watch for stringy chips and don’t clear them by hand.
    • Cork dust: wear a dust mask (N95 or better) and use dust collection; work outdoors or with a shop vacuum.
    • Epoxy: nitrile gloves and ventilation; solvents (alcohol) away from ignition sources; don’t use epoxy past its working time.
    • Anodizing chemicals (if home-done): acids, gloves, goggles, and disposal rules. To confirm whether I’d do that or send it out.
