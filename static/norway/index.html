Huts & Trout

Two weeks of hut-to-hut walking through Rondane and Jotunheimen, with a few unhurried dry-fly evenings on the trout and grayling rivers in between.

Norway, August 7–20, 2027. Two walkers, one vegetarian, no guides.

Why go

Norway does something rare: it lets you walk all day above the treeline, sleep in a warm staffed lodge with a hot dinner and a drying room, and wake up to do it again, with no tent and no stove. The Norwegian Trekking Association (DNT) has run this network for over 150 years, and the red-painted T on the cairns will keep you honest in fog.

The fishing is the other half of the argument. The big valley rivers, the Glomma system in the east and the Lågen/Otta/Sjoa system in the west, are wadeable, gin-clear and full of wild brown trout and grayling that rise to a dry fly. Licences are sold online by the day, and nobody is guarding a beat.

Best for
Hiking with a roof overhead, dry-fly trout and grayling, very long light.

About 16 hours on Aug 7, shrinking to about 14 by Aug 20 (inland south). Evenings get noticeably shorter by the end.
Temperatures
Valleys 15–22 °C by day. Plateaus 5–12 °C, with frost possible at night.

Assumptions

Dates are Aug 7–20, 2027 (this is being planned in Nov 2026). Flying in and out of Oslo, renting a car. Two experienced walkers, comfortable with 6–8 hour days. Everything marked VERIFY came from memory or thin sources and should be confirmed before you book. See Verify first.

When to go (and why mid-August)

Staffed DNT huts generally run from late June to early September, so mid-August sits comfortably in season. Snowfields on the high passes have mostly gone, the long evening light is still generous, and the worst of the mosquito season in the valleys has usually passed, though black flies and midges (knott) can linger near water. Expect a mix: a few glorious clear days, a couple of wet ones, and fog on the plateau at the worst moment.

The trade-off is crowds. Besseggen and the Gjende huts are the busiest places in the country in July and the first half of August. Crowds may thin once Norwegian schools restart in mid-August VERIFY. Book early either way, and consider walking against the grain.

On the fishing side, high summer means low, clear water and wary fish. Fish early and late, go small and light on the tippet, and expect the best dry-fly action at dusk when caddis come off.

The two-week route

The shape: Rondane huts → valley rest days with a little fishing → Jotunheimen huts → home. Fishing is limited to a handful of short, self-guided evening or morning sessions, mostly on the rest days between hut blocks. If the plateau is socked in, you swap a rest day forward.

Oslo to Rena

Land in Oslo, collect the car, shop for dry goods and gas canisters (you can't fly with them), and drive about three hours north into Østerdalen. Sleep near Rena.

Easy day, optional Glomma session

Sleep off the jet lag, then an optional evening on the Glomma near Rena or Rendalen with a day licence (fiskekort) from inatur.no. Easy wading, good for shaking out the rust. Check the minimum size on your ticket (25 cm was quoted for trout and grayling on the Glomma VERIFY).

Hike

Into Rondane

Drive to the Rondane trailhead and walk to Rondvassbu, the largest staffed hut in the park, beside the lake Rondvatnet. An easy first day under the shadow of Rondeslottet. Check the current trailhead, toll road and boat arrangements on ut.no VERIFY.

Hike

Rondeslottet or the high pass

Either a summit day on Rondeslottet (2,178 m, a long day) or the traverse towards Bjørnhollia, another staffed hut. Rondane's rounded ten-peaks-over-2,000 m skyline gives you huge views for relatively little exposure. Reindeer are common.

5Aug 11
Hike

Out to the valley

Walk out to the road. Rondane's staffed huts are Rondvassbu, Bjørnhollia and Dørålseter; the rest are self-service (key from DNT membership). Taxi or pre-arranged lift back to the car.

6Aug 12
Fish

Rest day near Otta

Hot shower, laundry, big breakfast. In the evening, an easy session on the Lågen/Otta confluence area on an inatur ticket. Brown trout and grayling both present. Evening rise is the thing. Sleep in Otta or Vinstra.

7Aug 13
Fish

Optional Sjoa morning, then to Gjendesheim

If you feel like it, an early hour on the Sjoa, a fast, pretty river that drains Gjende. Otherwise just drive up to Gjendesheim (staffed, the east end of Lake Gjende) and sleep there ready for the big day.

8Aug 14
Hike

Besseggen to Memurubu

The famous ridge, with the lake Gjende (a vivid green) on one side and Bessvatnet (deep blue) on the other. Allow 6–8 hours. Start early to beat the queues at the scrambly bits. Sleep at Memurubu.

9Aug 15
Hike

Memurubu to Gjendebu

Take the lake boat or walk the trail along the north shore. Gjendebu sits at the west end, at the mouth of the Memurudalen/Svartdalen valleys. A short day is deliberate; use the spare hours to rest or cast a fly into the lake outlet if the licence allows.

Gjendebu to Glitterheim

Up and over into the heart of the park towards Glitterheim, below Glittertind. Roughly 5–7 hours; check time and route on ut.no VERIFY.

Glitterheim to Spiterstulen

The high-country finale. Spiterstulen is the base for Galdhøpiggen (2,469 m, Norway's highest). A fine place to end a week of hut walking, with a sauna on some evenings in season VERIFY.

Bøvra and the Otta at Lom

Bus or taxi down to Lom (stave church, bakery, outdoor shop). Fish the Bøvra or the Otta river in the evening. Sleep in Lom.

Lom to Oslo

Drive back via Valdres (about 5–6 hours), with an optional last session on the Begna if time and water allow. Sleep in Oslo.

If the weather turns

Days 6–7 and 12 are your flex days. Rondane and Jotunheimen both turn dangerous in low cloud, wind and sleet. Swap a fishing day forward and delay the hut leg; DNT staff will tell you the truth about conditions.

Hut-to-hut: how it works

DNT cabins come in three flavours: staffed (beds, dinner, breakfast, packed lunch, a shop for chocolate and a few essentials), self-service (bring your own food, use the provided stock and pay by the honour system), and unstaffed (basic). In August, staffed huts are a joy and fill up.

  • Join DNT first. Members get cheaper beds and, with a key, access to the self-service cabins. Membership is also what protects your trip if you need to be rescued off a bad pass.
  • Bring a sleeping liner (a "hut sleeper"). Bedding is supplied, but a sleeping bag is not needed.
  • Reserve beds as soon as the 2027 season opens at hyttebestilling.dnt.no. Gjendesheim, Memurubu and Gjendebu go first.
  • Price check: budget a few thousand NOK per person for the four hut nights plus dinner and breakfast, and treat that as a rough estimate VERIFY.
  • The trails are marked with red T's on stones. Carry a paper map as well as your phone; fog erases GPS faith.
  • Water: stream water is generally drinkable at altitude; fill up near the source, not below huts or grazing.

Eating: one vegetarian in the party

Staffed DNT huts run on a set menu: a hot multi-course dinner served at a fixed time, a buffet breakfast, and a packed lunch you make yourself from the breakfast spread. Traditional hut menus lean on meat, fish and dairy (reindeer stew, salmon, cheese, brunost), so a vegetarian needs to plan rather than hope.

What we actually know

Searches turned up no published vegetarian policy for Rondvassbu, Bjørnhollia, Gjendesheim, Memurubu, Gjendebu, Glitterheim or Spiterstulen. DNT does say staffed cabins serve a three-course dinner built on local ingredients, and Norwegian huts generally cook for guests who arrive unannounced. Whether any given hut can do a proper vegetarian dinner, and how much notice it wants, is VERIFY hut by hut.

How to de-risk the huts

  • Say it at booking. Use the notes field when reserving through hyttebestilling.dnt.no, naming the person, the dates and whether fish, eggs and dairy are fine.
  • Then email or call each hut a week or two before and ask for written confirmation. Huts buy in food for the days ahead, and the remote ones get supplies by pack horse, helicopter or boat, so late requests are hard.
  • Decide what "vegetarian" means for you. Many kitchens treat it as "no meat, fish is fine". If fish is out, say so clearly. If dairy and eggs are fine, the hut's job gets much easier.
  • Expect variation. The bigger, road-accessible lodges (Gjendesheim, Spiterstulen, Rondvassbu) are likely easier than the remote ones (Glitterheim, Gjendebu), but that is an educated guess, not a finding.
  • Breakfast and lunch are the easy part. Bread, cheese, eggs, muesli, yoghurt, peanut butter and fruit are standard. Dinner is the pinch point.

The backup larder

Carry a modest emergency stash for the four hut nights (days 3–5 and 8–11) so a mediocre dinner never becomes an empty stomach:

  • Nut butter, nuts, seeds and dried fruit.
  • Instant lentil, bean or couscous packets, or a dried hummus mix.
  • Protein: vegetarian jerky or bars, powdered milk or protein powder.
  • Instant noodles or soup with a vegetarian stock (check labels).
  • Staffed huts sell chocolate, crisps and a few basics, but don't count on them for real meals.

Self-service huts (Rondane has several) mean cooking your own, which is easy for a vegetarian: they stock basic dry goods you pay for on the honour system, and you bring the rest.

Off the mountain

Valley days are easy. Otta, Vinstra, Lom and Rena have supermarkets (Coop, Rema 1000 and Kiwi are everywhere) with plenty of vegetarian staples, and most towns have at least one pizzeria or café with a meat-free option. Stock the backup larder on day 1 in Oslo, where vegetarian choice is wide.

Fishing note

If one of you doesn't eat fish, nothing about the plan changes: catch-and-release with barbless hooks is the norm, and rules for taking fish out of the country are tight anyway. If the other does want a trout dinner, ask a hut or guesthouse whether they will cook your catch before you assume it.

Fishing: trout and grayling, not salmon

The quarry

Brown trout (ørret) and grayling (harr), mostly wild, and both willing to take dry flies. The sweet spot is a long, clean drift on a cloudy afternoon or at dusk. Grayling fishing improves in September and October, so an August trip will find them a bit less showy but still present.

Licences and rules

  • Licence: freshwater fishing requires a local fiskekort, issued by the landowner or fishing association. Buy it online at inatur.no, or at sports shops, convenience stores and campsites. It's valid only for the stated water and period.
  • State fishing fee (fiskeravgift): this applies to salmon, sea trout and sea char. For inland trout and grayling it should not be needed VERIFY.
  • Disinfect your gear. Norway is fighting the parasite Gyrodactylus salaris, and gear used abroad generally needs to be disinfected or fully dried before you fish. Some local areas require disinfection when you leave as well. The simplest approach is to treat waders, boots and nets before you fly, then again at the end VERIFY.
  • Size limits and bag limits vary by water. Read the ticket. Catch-and-release with barbless hooks is welcome almost everywhere.
  • Taking fish home: rules for foreign anglers exporting catch have been tightened in recent years. Plan to eat what you catch, not carry it home VERIFY.

Tackle and flies

  • Rod: 9 ft #4 or #5 for rivers. A #3 for the smallest streams.
  • Leader: 12 ft tapered to 5X, longer in clear low water.
  • Dry flies: Elk Hair Caddis, Klinkhammer, Parachute Adams, CDC patterns and small beetles in 12–16.
  • Nymphs: Pheasant Tail, Hare's Ear and a black bead-head for deeper runs.
  • Extras: head net or repellent (midges), polarised glasses, a thermos, and a wading staff for the cold glacial rivers.
  • Packing the rod on a hut trek: don't. Leave the fishing gear in the car, and walk with trekking kit only. The fishing blocks and hut blocks are separate on purpose.

Oslo bookends

The plan has one real evening in Oslo, the night of day 13 (Aug 19), plus whatever is left of the arrival and departure days. If you want more, arrive a day early, or trade the easy day on day 2 for a city day. Finishing in Trondheim removes the Oslo night entirely.

Practical

Getting there and around

Fly into Oslo (OSL) and rent a car; the fishing days make a car worth it. Between trailheads you can use buses and the lake boat on Gjende, but timetables are thin and seasonal. Book the car and the boat times once the huts are fixed.

Right to roam

Norway's allemannsretten lets you walk and camp on uncultivated land, but you should keep well away from houses, close gates and take your rubbish with you. Fishing is not covered by it: you still need a licence. As a fallback if a hut falls through, the rule of thumb is a tent at least 150 m from the nearest inhabited house, for no more than two nights in one spot VERIFY. That means carrying tent gear, so it is a last resort.

Safety

  • Emergency number: 112. Reception is patchy on the plateau.
  • Forecast: yr.no, from the Norwegian Meteorological Institute. Check it the night before and again at breakfast.
  • The Norwegian Mountain Code, in short: plan the trip and tell someone the route; adapt it to your ability and the conditions; watch the weather; expect bad weather and frost even on short trips; carry what you need to help yourself and others; use a map and compass; and turn back without shame.
  • Leave a route and return time with someone, and tell the hut staff where you're headed next.
  • Pack real waterproofs, a warm layer, gloves and a hat, even in August.
  • River crossings can be dangerous after rain or snowmelt; use bridges where they exist.

Packing short list

  • Vegetarian backup larder (see Eating), hut sleeper, headlamp, small towel, earplugs.
  • Rain jacket and trousers, fleece, base layers, warm hat and gloves.
  • Broken-in boots and a pair of camp shoes.
  • Paper map, compass, power bank.
  • Compact fishing kit (stays in the car during hut blocks), wading boots without felt if you can manage it.

Appendix: Lofoten, for a later trip

A friend of a friend passed on a Lofoten guide and some personal tips. Lofoten is not part of this plan: it is far north, built around day hikes and the coast rather than huts, and the midnight sun there runs from about May 28 to July 14, so mid-August nights are dark again. It makes a good trip of its own. These notes are here so they are not lost.

The guide

Lofoten guide by Helene Myhre (@helenemoo), 94 pages, © 2022, with a linked Google Maps list of its spots. It is her work, so it is not reproduced here; the PDF stays in Downloads.

  • Peak season is crowded. The guide advises booking accommodation, rental cars and flights well ahead, because prices climb fast.
  • Rules have tightened. In 2021 parts of Haukland beach and Reinebringen were closed to tents because of the pressure on the land. Check the current rules.
  • Gear can be borrowed or rented. The guide points to second-hand sites and to BUA, a free equipment loan in Kabelvåg.
  • Food: the author eats mostly vegetables and legumes, and says there are good meat-free options. She links a vegan food guide for Lofoten in Norwegian. Useful for a vegetarian traveller.
  • Leave no trace: carry out all waste, stay on the trail, respect wildlife.
